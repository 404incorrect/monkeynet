😄 Deep Roots

Apes and humans have been laughing the same way for fifteen million years

University of Warwick · 2026

Researchers studying great apes, orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, and bonobos, found their laughter shares the same underlying rhythmic structure as human laughter. The pattern likely sat in a common ancestor some fifteen million years ago and has barely changed since. Long before language, before fire, before anyone built a canopy out of anything, something was already funny, and somebody was already laughing about it in roughly the cadence we still use. That's a long time to keep a joke running.

🏛️ Policy

A bill in Congress would stop importing monkeys for biomedical research

United States · Congress · introduced July 10, 2026

U.S. Representative Greg Steube of Florida introduced bipartisan legislation, the PRIMATE Act, to prohibit importing non-human primates into the country for biomedical research. It follows USDA citations against a Florida facility. A bill introduced is not a bill passed, and there's a long climb between the two. But the premise underneath it is the same one we keep coming back to: a monkey is not raw material. That the idea has a bill number now, and names from more than one party on it, is worth saying out loud.

🩺 Reform

More than 550 doctors want primate testing phased out for human-based methods

United States · HHS · July 10, 2026

More than 550 physicians signed on to a call urging Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to phase out experimentation on primates in favor of modern, human-based research methods. It lands alongside the bipartisan PRIMATE Act now before Congress, two pushes on the same idea from different directions. The case the doctors are making is partly scientific and partly the older one we keep coming back to: that there are better ways to learn, and that a monkey is not the only instrument available. Worth watching whether it moves anyone with the power to act on it.

🌿 Retirement

A hundred and thirty retired research macaques are looking for a better branch to land on

United States · CDC · reopened June 20, reported July 1, 2026

After public pushback on a plan to send its retired rhesus and pigtail macaques to a single Texas sanctuary, the CDC reopened the process, posting a revised contract opportunity for facilities to bid on housing the 130 animals. It's paperwork, technically. But underneath the paperwork is a straightforward question about where a monkey who has already given a working life gets to spend the rest of it, and whether we can manage to get that part right. Worth watching where they end up.

Seattle's canopy gets its first colobus in twenty-nine years, arriving all in white

Woodland Park Zoo, Seattle · born June 15, announced June 30, 2026

A black-and-white colobus monkey was born to parents Mokiki and Lewis, the first colobus the zoo has welcomed in twenty-nine years. Newborns arrive pure white and darken into their adult coloring over their first three months, so for a little while there's a small bright thing up in the branches that hasn't figured out yet how much it's going to change. 🐒 Jailbreak

Nearly a hundred macaques let themselves out, and it's hard to blame them

Lopburi, Thailand · June 30, 2026

Overnight, close to a hundred long-tailed macaques broke out of the Lopburi Municipality Animal Nursery, sending staff scrambling at dawn to round them back up while somehow keeping the other thousand-plus residents from getting the same idea. Officials didn't dress up the why: hunger, heat, and too many bodies in too little space. A canopy is supposed to have room in it, and when it doesn't, this is what happens, animals voting with their feet the only way they can. The city says a sturdier, double-layer enclosure is on the way. Good. Build them somewhere worth staying.

✋ Hands Off

Someone pointed a laser at a baby monkey for the feed. The zoo is done asking nicely.

Ichikawa City Zoo, Japan · June 25, 2026

Punch, a baby monkey who'd gone viral, had a laser aimed into his enclosure by a visitor who then posted the clip online. Zoo officials warned it can cause real stress and even blindness, and said anyone caught doing it will be ejected on the spot. This is the extractive feed logic followed all the way to its meanest end: a living thing turned into raw material for a video. A monkey is not content. Punch is not content. Put the laser away.

The Canopy Itself

Four days of rain took roughly seven percent of the rarest great ape alive

Batang Toru, Indonesia · study published June 16, 2026

A study in Current Biology found that four days of extreme rain from Cyclone Senyar triggered landslides that killed an estimated 58 Tapanuli orangutans, a critically endangered great ape with fewer than 800 individuals left in the world. Not a monkey; a cousin, up in the same trees. There isn't a lighter way to hold a number like that, so we won't try. The canopy we like to talk about as a metaphor is, for them, an actual forest, and it is getting harder to live in. We're just here to make sure the number gets said out loud.

🍌 Heist

One monkey, one neem tree, and a thirty-minute audit of the fiat system

Bulandshahr, Uttar Pradesh, India · May 31, 2026

Outside a courthouse, a monkey lifted a lawyer's bag holding roughly ₹2 lakh, carried it up a neem tree, and for about half an hour simply rained the currency notes back down while a crowd below scrambled to catch them. No demands, no manifesto. Just a small primate at the top of a tree, quietly demonstrating that a banknote is only worth something if everyone underneath agrees to keep chasing it. The video, predictably, went everywhere.

🌿 Comeback

Singapore counted its rarest leaf-monkeys again, and there are twice as many

Singapore · May 2026

New survey data shows the population of the Raffles' banded langur, a critically endangered leaf-eating monkey found in only a thin band of forest across Singapore and southern Malaysia, has doubled. Doubling sounds bigger than the numbers underneath it, this is still a small and fragile group, but it moved in the right direction, and it moved because people decided it should: protected forest, planted corridors, years of quiet patient work. Not every canopy story is a rescue after the fact. Some are just this, a count going up because somebody bothered.

Trafficking

A proboscis monkey turned up in Thailand, far from the only island that is home

Thailand · April 2026

An injured proboscis monkey, a species that lives nowhere on earth but Borneo, was found near a railway track in Thailand. It needed a finger and part of its tail amputated. There was no CITES paper trail explaining how it crossed a sea and a border, and experts say that absence points to one thing: illegal trade. A canopy is somewhere you climb because you want to. This is the opposite of that: a small life moved against its will, and hurt on the way. We're glad it was found. We wish it had never had to be.

🐒 New Arrival

A critically endangered gibbon was born in England, and named Lima

Port Lympne, England · April 2026

Port Lympne wild animal park welcomed a baby Javan gibbon named Lima, born to mother Belle. There are only a few thousand Javan gibbons left in the wild, spread across the shrinking forests of a single island, so every new one is both an ordinary miracle and a small hedge against a bad ending. Gibbons spend their whole lives swinging hand over hand through the high branches. Lima has all of that still ahead. Welcome, and go far.
